## Unlocked Combinations

Both combinations considered for this chapter have all their prerequisites in earlier chapters, so both are released, each with a lesson and a ladder of its own.

### Teach Now

A graph read together with a heap is the seventh lesson. Relaxation over the graph creates improved tentative costs, the heap exposes the smallest candidate, and rejecting a stale entry stands in for lowering a key that Java's queue cannot lower. The ladder changes what a path is worth: a network where the last listener is reported, a route judged by its hardest step, flights with a limit on legs that needs a state of its own, and a probability that is multiplied and maximised. The false friend is one distance per node, which discards an arrival that is dearer but better placed.

A graph read together with a deque is the eighth lesson. The graph supplies edges whose cost is only zero or one, and the deque keeps tentative costs in order by taking a free step at the front and a paid step at the back, so no heap is needed. The ladder moves from the pure drill of choosing an end, through arrows in a grid and obstacles on a board, to a toll map. The false friend is plain breadth-first search, which counts edges and ignores the difference between free and paid.

### Deferred

Negative weights, all-pairs distances and heuristics that guide the search toward a target need other arguments, namely repeated relaxation with a cycle check, a table over pairs and an admissible estimate. They belong to Chapter 25, so no problem of those kinds is assigned here.

### Already Covered

Unit-cost search, layers and state encodings come from Chapters 21 and 22. The heap and its comparator come from Chapter 17, the double-ended queue from Chapter 13, and the edge-ordering idea used by spanning trees was taught in Chapter 23.
